## Source hierarchy

Rank evidence by fit, not prestige alone:

1. Direct customer behavior, transactions, product usage, official records, or primary interviews
2. Current regulatory, company, standards, or first-party product sources
3. Transparent research with definitions, sample, period, and methodology
4. Reputable analysis that links to underlying evidence
5. Vendor claims, aggregators, anonymous commentary, or search snippets

Record why a lower-tier source is still useful. Never let its confidence exceed its evidence.

## Source lineage and time

- Group syndications, press rewrites, mirrors, and summaries of the same announcement, dataset, or study into one evidence family.
- Record the publisher, canonical URL, and the exact locator that supports a material claim: heading, table, page, dataset field, release section, or commit.
- Keep publication, update, event, observation or measurement, retrieval, and requested as-of dates separate. A later retrospective source can explain an earlier state but does not prove what was publicly available at that time.
- Use evidence status: supports, contradicts, unavailable, or blocked. Not found or inaccessible is not evidence of nonexistence.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Include the status quo, manual work, internal build, and doing nothing when they are real alternatives.

## Confidence scale

- **High:** direct, current, definition-aligned evidence with independent corroboration
- **Medium:** useful evidence with a known sample, recency, definition, or incentive limitation
- **Low:** directional, indirect, vendor-provided, stale, or weakly defined evidence
- **Unknown:** required evidence was not obtained

## Decision memo

1. State the decision and recommendation.
2. Name the strongest supporting and contradicting evidence.
3. Separate facts, estimates, and inference.
4. Explain source conflicts and residual uncertainty.
5. Record rejected options and downside risk.
6. End with the smallest validation step that could change the recommendation.
